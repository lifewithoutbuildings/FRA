## 1. Agent vs principal

A ticket aggregator sells a flight ticket:

| Party | What it sells | When revenue is recognised | Amount |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Aggregator** *(agent)* | Booking service | At the point of sale | Only its **commission / convenience fee** — net |
| **Airline** *(principal)* | Carriage | Only once the **flight has flown** | Full fare — gross |

> [!important] Two separate performance obligations
> The aggregator's obligation is discharged the moment the booking is confirmed. The airline's is discharged only when it actually flies you. Until then the fare sits on the airline's balance sheet as unearned revenue (the "air traffic liability") — the same [[Unearned Revenue|deferred revenue]] mechanic as the gift card in [[FRA Session 9]].

> [!tip] Why gross vs net matters
> Booking gross when you're an agent inflates the top line without touching profit. Classic dot-com-era aggressiveness — and the reason the standard tests **control** of the good or service before transfer, not just who collects the cash.

> [!note] Read the balance sheet first
> Of $37bn of assets, **$17bn is cash and short-term investments** and a further $14bn is equity investments. Property and equipment is $1.6bn. Almost nothing on this balance sheet is the actual business — the software franchise that generates $19.7bn of revenue is **entirely absent as an asset**.
>
> Also note **unearned revenue of $4,239m**, growing from $1,418m in 1997 — the [[Revenue Recognition|deferral]] mechanic from [[FRA Session 9]] showing up as the single largest operating liability.

> [!warning] Book value ≠ asset value
> Book equity of $28bn against a market capitalisation running into the hundreds of billions. The gap is the internally generated intangible that accounting refuses to recognise, because there is no reliable, verifiable cost basis for it.

### The accounting rule

> Costs incurred internally in creating a software product shall be charged to expense when incurred as research and development until **technological feasibility** has been established for the product. Technological feasibility is established upon completion of a detail program design or, in its absence, completion of a working model. Thereafter, all software production costs shall be capitalized and subsequently reported at the lower of unamortized cost or net realizable value.

Microsoft's own 1999 policy note: R&D costs are expensed as incurred; **SFAS 86** does not materially [affect the Company] — i.e. management's position is that the window between technological feasibility and general release is short enough that capitalisable amounts are immaterial.

## 3. In-class exercise — capitalise 60% of R&D

### Step 0 — Assumptions

| Item | Assumption |
| :--- | :--- |
| Capitalisation rate | 60% of reported R&D capitalised; 40% expensed |
| Useful life | **2 years**, straight-line |
| Convention | Amortisation begins in the year of capitalisation (½ per year) |
| Taxes | **No tax adjustment** — restatement carried through pre-tax |

> [!note] Why the convention matters
> With a 2-year life and half amortised in the year of spend, each layer is fully written off by the end of the following year. Only the **current year's** layer survives on the balance sheet at year-end.

### Step 1 — The question being tested

Microsoft expenses **all** R&D. The exercise asks the opposite: what if a substantial share of that spend genuinely creates a multi-period asset?

### Step 2 — Split reported R&D 60/40 ($m)

| Year | Reported R&D | Capitalised (60%) | Expensed (40%) |
| :--- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| 1995 | 860 | 516.0 | 344.0 |
| 1996 | 1,326 | 795.6 | 530.4 |
| 1997 | 1,863 | 1,117.8 | 745.2 |
| 1998 | 2,601 | 1,560.6 | 1,040.4 |
| 1999 | 2,970 | 1,782.0 | 1,188.0 |

### Step 3 — Amortisation schedule (2-year life)

$$\text{Amortisation}_t = \tfrac{1}{2}\left(\text{Cap}_t + \text{Cap}_{t-1}\right)$$

| Year | Layers amortised | Calculation | Amortisation |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | ---: |
| 1997 | 1997 + 1996 | ½(1,117.8 + 795.6) | **956.7** |
| 1998 | 1998 + 1997 | ½(1,560.6 + 1,117.8) | **1,339.2** |
| 1999 | 1999 + 1998 | ½(1,782.0 + 1,560.6) | **1,671.3** |

### Step 4 — Restated income statement ($m)

Adjusted R&D charge = 40% expensed + amortisation of capitalised layers.

| | 1997 | 1998 | 1999 |
| :--- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Reported R&D | 1,863.0 | 2,601.0 | 2,970.0 |
| 40% expensed | 745.2 | 1,040.4 | 1,188.0 |
| Amortisation | 956.7 | 1,339.2 | 1,671.3 |
| **Adjusted R&D charge** | **1,701.9** | **2,379.6** | **2,859.3** |
| **Income effect** | **+161.1** | **+221.4** | **+110.7** |
| Reported operating income | 4,871.0 | 6,414.0 | 9,928.0 |
| **Adjusted operating income** | **5,032.1** | **6,635.4** | **10,038.7** |
| Reported net income | 3,454.0 | 4,490.0 | 7,785.0 |
| **Adjusted net income** | **3,615.1** | **4,711.4** | **7,895.7** |
| Change in net income | +4.7% | +4.9% | +1.4% |

> [!important] The mechanism
> Income rises **only because R&D spending is growing**. Capitalised (1,782.0) exceeds amortised (1,671.3) in 1999, so a net 110.7 is deferred to the balance sheet.
>
> The gap is narrow because a 2-year life forces rapid write-off. R&D growth also decelerated sharply — 39.6% in 1998 versus 14.2% in 1999 — which is why the earnings uplift collapses from 4.9% to 1.4%. **In a steady state with flat R&D, the effect on income would be exactly zero.**

### Step 5 — Restated balance sheet ($m)

Under a 2-year life with half-year-of-spend amortisation, the year-end net asset is the unamortised half of the current year's layer:

$$\text{Net capitalised software} = \tfrac{1}{2} \times \text{Cap}_t$$

| | 1997 | 1998 | 1999 |
| :--- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Capitalised software, net | 558.9 | 780.3 | 891.0 |
| Retained earnings uplift | 558.9 | 780.3 | 891.0 |

With no tax adjustment, the asset increase flows entirely to equity — liabilities are untouched.

**Adjusted FY1999 position**

| | Reported | Adjustment | Adjusted |
| :--- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Total assets | 37,156 | +891.0 | **38,047.0** |
| Total liabilities | 8,718 | — | **8,718.0** |
| Total equity | 28,438 | +891.0 | **29,329.0** |

> [!check] Articulation check
> Δ net asset 1999 = 891.0 − 780.3 = **110.7** = the income effect ✓
> Δ net asset 1998 = 780.3 − 558.9 = **221.4** ✓
> Assets 38,047.0 = Liabilities 8,718.0 + Equity 29,329.0 ✓

### Step 6 — Ratio impact (FY1999, ending balances)

| Ratio | Reported | Adjusted | Direction |
| :--- | ---: | ---: | :--- |
| Net margin | 39.4% | 40.0% | ↑ |
| ROA | 21.0% | 20.8% | ↓ |
| ROE | 27.4% | 26.9% | ↓ |
| R&D as % of revenue (charged) | 15.0% | 14.5% | ↓ |

> [!warning] Counter-intuitive result
> Capitalisation raises net income by 1.4% but raises assets by 2.4% and equity by 3.1%. Because the **denominators grow faster than the numerator**, ROA and ROE both fall.
>
> Capitalisation flatters the P&L and dilutes measured returns — it does not make the business look more profitable on a returns basis.

### Step 7 — Conclusions

1. **The adjustment is small.** A 2-year life produces a peak balance sheet asset of $891m against $37.2bn of reported assets — roughly 2.4%. Net income moves less than 1.5% in 1999.
2. **The effect is a growth artefact.** The entire income uplift equals the excess of current capitalisation over current amortisation. That excess exists only while R&D spend is rising, and it shrinks as growth decelerates.
3. **Useful life drives everything.** Moving from a 3-year to a 2-year life cuts the 1999 income effect from 295.2 to 110.7 — a 62% reduction — and cuts the net asset from 1,708.2 to 891.0. The answer is highly sensitive to an assumption with no objective anchor.
4. **Earnings quality direction.** Expensing all R&D is the **conservative** treatment. Restating to capitalisation makes reported earnings *less* conservative — which is why cross-company comparisons should be normalised to one policy rather than treating either presentation as "correct."

> [!question]- Discussion points to be ready for
> - If capitalisation barely moves the numbers, why does the standard exist at all? *(Comparability across firms whose feasibility-to-release windows differ — pharma and hardware, not Microsoft.)*
> - Why would Microsoft *prefer* to expense? Conservative earnings build a cushion; deferring recognition when you're growing 30%+ costs nothing you need.
> - What breaks if the useful life is wrong? An overstated life leaves a stale asset on the books that eventually needs impairment — the failure mode is a large one-off write-down, not a smooth mis-statement.
