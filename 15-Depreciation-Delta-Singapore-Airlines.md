> [!abstract] In one line
> Two airlines, same aircraft, **wildly different depreciation** — because useful life and salvage value are **management estimates**, not facts. The case teaches how that discretion moves reported earnings, why comparability needs the footnotes, and how Singapore's aggressive short-life policy feeds its **gain-on-sale** strategy.

> [!info] Why this case matters for the quiz
> It is the course's headline example of **managerial discretion** (course thrust #3). The examinable skill is the **depreciation-per-$100-of-gross-value** calculation and the *reasoning* about why firms choose different assumptions. Full case + exhibits: [[Delta Airlines Case]].

## 1. Depreciation per $100 of gross aircraft value

> [!important] The comparability device
> To compare two firms cleanly, strip out fleet size: compute annual depreciation on **$100 of gross aircraft cost**.
> $$\text{Dep per \$100} = \frac{100 \times (1 - \text{salvage \%})}{\text{depreciable life}}$$

| Company / period | Life | Salvage | Dep per $100 |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| **Singapore — before Apr 1989** | 8 yr | 10% | $11.25 |
| **Singapore — after Apr 1989** | 10 yr | 20% | **$8.00** |
| **Delta — before Jul 1986** | 10 yr | 10% | $9.00 |
| **Delta — Jul 1986 → Mar 1993** | 15 yr | 10% | $6.00 |
| **Delta — from Apr 1993** | 20 yr | 5% | **$4.75** |

> [!tip] The mental model — two levers, one direction
> Depreciation has exactly **two management levers**: **useful life** (longer → less per year) and **salvage %** (higher → less to write off). Push either the "profit-flattering" way and **annual depreciation falls → reported profit rises now.** That's precisely what *both* airlines did over time, and what Delta did again in 1993 (life 15→20, salvage 10%→5%). The catch is deferred, not avoided: lower depreciation now means **higher book values** later, so **less gain (or more loss) when the asset is sold** — unless, like Singapore, you depreciate *hard* and sell young to bank the gain. So when you see a life/salvage change, ask two things: **which way does profit move now?** and **what does it do to future gain-on-sale?**

> [!note] Read the trend
> **Both airlines lengthened lives over time → lower depreciation → higher reported profit.** By 1993 Delta charges **$4.75** where Singapore charges **$8.00** for the identical $100 of aircraft — Delta books **barely more than half** the depreciation.

## 2. The five questions — answered

> [!question]- Q1 — Depreciation per $100 (see table above)
> Delta: $9.00 → $6.00 → **$4.75**. Singapore: $11.25 → **$8.00** (new aircraft). The 1993 gap: Singapore $8.00 vs Delta $4.75.

> [!question]- Q2 — Are the differences significant? Is different treatment proper?
> **Yes, significant.** Depreciation is a large operating expense (Singapore ~16% of operating expense vs Delta ~5%), so the assumption swings reported profit materially. It **is proper** *provided each set of estimates honestly reflects that firm's own fleet plan* — Singapore genuinely flies younger aircraft and sells them early; Delta genuinely holds aircraft longer. The concern is **comparability**: an analyst must normalise to one policy (via the footnotes) before comparing the two.

> [!question]- Q3 — How much did Delta's 1993 change matter? Vs Singapore's assumptions?
> Delta moved from **$6.00 → $4.75** per $100, i.e. **$1.25 less** depreciation per $100 of gross aircraft.
> Average FY1993 gross flight equipment ≈ (9,043 + 8,354)/2 ≈ **$8,700m**.
> - **Effect of Delta's own change:** 8,700 × 1.25/100 ≈ **$109m less** annual depreciation (→ that much *more* pre-tax profit) — of a piece with the **$130m** the 1986 change saved (Exhibit 4).
> - **Delta vs Singapore's assumptions ($8.00):** difference $8.00 − $4.75 = $3.25 per $100 → 8,700 × 3.25/100 ≈ **$283m more** depreciation *if* Delta used Singapore's policy. On a 1993 net loss of $(415)m, that is enormous.

> [!question]- Q4 — What does Singapore gain/lose by different assumptions? Link to strategy.
> Shorter life + higher salvage = **higher depreciation now → lower reported profit now** (a conservative, cushion-building P&L). What it *gains*: its strategy is to **turn the fleet over quickly**. Because it depreciates hard, book values are low, so when it **sells young aircraft it books large gains on sale** (Exhibit 5: gains of $42–208m/yr), and it flies the **youngest fleet in the world** (5.1 yrs) — a service/brand advantage for business travellers. Heavy depreciation is the accounting counterpart of a fast-turnover, premium-service strategy.

> [!question]- Q5 — Does fleet age affect depreciation expense?
> **Not the per-$100 *rate*** — that's set by the life/salvage assumption, independent of age. But age affects the **stock of depreciation already taken**: an older fleet (Delta 8.8 yrs) is further through its schedule, so more is already accumulated and *annual* charges on fully-depreciated planes stop. A younger fleet (Singapore 5.1 yrs) still has most depreciation ahead of it. So age shifts *where in the schedule* each firm sits, not the rate per $100.

## Traps

> [!warning]
> - **Comparing raw depreciation $** between the two firms — always normalise **per $100 of gross value** first.
> - **Forgetting salvage %** in the per-$100 formula — it's $(1-\text{salvage})$ in the numerator.
> - **Calling either policy "wrong"** — both are proper; the issue is comparability, resolved via footnotes.
> - **Missing the strategy link** — heavy depreciation → low book value → **big gain on sale** → supports fast fleet turnover.

## Key takeaways

- Dep per $100 = $100(1−salvage)/life. 1993: **Delta $4.75 vs Singapore $8.00**.
- Delta's 1993 change: ~**$109m** less depreciation; using Singapore's policy would add ~**$283m**.
- Life & salvage are **estimates** → managerial discretion → the reason footnotes exist.
- Singapore's aggressive depreciation underwrites a **young-fleet, gain-on-sale** strategy.
