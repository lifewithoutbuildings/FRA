> [!abstract] In one line
> Two airlines, same aircraft, **wildly different depreciation** — because useful life and salvage value are **management estimates**, not facts. The case teaches how that discretion moves reported earnings, why comparability needs the footnotes, and how Singapore's aggressive short-life policy feeds its **gain-on-sale** strategy.

This is the course's headline example of **managerial discretion** (course thrust #3). The examinable skill is the depreciation-per-$100-of-gross-value calculation together with the *reasoning* about why two firms in one industry choose different assumptions. Full case and exhibits: [[Delta Airlines Case]].

---

## 1. Depreciation per $100 of gross aircraft value

Fleet sizes differ, so raw depreciation dollars can't be compared. Strip out size by computing annual depreciation on **$100 of gross aircraft cost** — a like-for-like rate that depends only on the two estimates each airline chooses:

$$\text{Dep per \$100} = \frac{100 \times (1 - \text{salvage \%})}{\text{depreciable life}}$$

| Company / period | Life | Salvage | Dep per $100 |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| **Singapore — before Apr 1989** | 8 yr | 10% | $11.25 |
| **Singapore — after Apr 1989** | 10 yr | 20% | **$8.00** |
| **Delta — before Jul 1986** | 10 yr | 10% | $9.00 |
| **Delta — Jul 1986 → Mar 1993** | 15 yr | 10% | $6.00 |
| **Delta — from Apr 1993** | 20 yr | 5% | **$4.75** |

The trend runs one way for both: each airline lengthened lives over time, cutting depreciation and lifting reported profit. By 1993 Delta charges just **$4.75** where Singapore charges **$8.00** for the identical $100 of aircraft — barely more than half the depreciation.

## 2. The case questions, answered

**Q1 — Depreciation per $100.** Delta steps down $9.00 → $6.00 → **$4.75**; Singapore $11.25 → **$8.00** on new aircraft. The 1993 comparison is Singapore $8.00 against Delta $4.75 (the table above does the arithmetic).

**Q2 — Are the differences significant, and is the different treatment proper?** Significant, yes: depreciation is a large operating expense — about 16% of operating expense at Singapore against roughly 5% at Delta — so the assumption swings reported profit materially. And it is proper, *provided* each set of estimates honestly reflects that firm's own fleet plan: Singapore genuinely flies younger aircraft and sells them early, Delta genuinely holds its aircraft longer. The real concern isn't propriety but **comparability** — an analyst has to normalise both firms to one policy, using the footnotes, before setting their numbers side by side.

**Q3 — How much did Delta's 1993 change matter, and how much versus Singapore's assumptions?** Delta's own change moved it from $6.00 to $4.75 per $100, i.e. **$1.25 less** depreciation per $100 of gross aircraft. On average FY1993 gross flight equipment of about (9,043 + 8,354)/2 ≈ **$8,700m**, that is 8,700 × 1.25/100 ≈ **$109m less** annual depreciation — and so that much *more* pre-tax profit, of a piece with the **$130m** the 1986 change saved (Exhibit 4). Measured instead against Singapore's policy, the gap is $8.00 − $4.75 = $3.25 per $100, or 8,700 × 3.25/100 ≈ **$283m more** depreciation *if* Delta depreciated the way Singapore does — enormous against Delta's 1993 net loss of $(415)m.

**Q4 — What does Singapore gain or lose, and how does it link to strategy?** A shorter life and higher salvage mean **higher depreciation now, lower reported profit now** — a conservative, cushion-building P&L. What Singapore gains is the payoff of a fast-turnover strategy: because it depreciates hard, book values stay low, so when it sells its young aircraft it books **large gains on sale** (Exhibit 5: $42–208m a year) and flies the **youngest fleet in the world** (5.1 years), a service and brand advantage aimed at business travellers. The heavy depreciation is simply the accounting counterpart of a fast-turnover, premium-service strategy.

**Q5 — Does fleet age affect depreciation expense?** Not the per-$100 *rate* — that is fixed by the life-and-salvage assumption, independent of how old the planes are. What age changes is the *stock* of depreciation already taken: an older fleet (Delta, 8.8 years) is further through its schedule, so more is already accumulated and the annual charge on any fully-depreciated plane has stopped, while a younger fleet (Singapore, 5.1 years) still has most of its depreciation ahead. So age shifts *where in the schedule* each firm sits, not the rate per $100.

## Before the quiz

- Never compare **raw depreciation dollars** between the two firms — normalise **per $100 of gross value** first, and don't drop the $(1-\text{salvage})$ from the numerator.
- Neither policy is "wrong"; both are proper, and the issue they raise is **comparability**, resolved through the footnotes. Life and salvage are **estimates** — this is exactly why managerial discretion matters and why footnotes exist.
- 1993 headline: **Delta $4.75 vs Singapore $8.00**; Delta's own change freed ~**$109m** of profit, and matching Singapore's policy would add ~**$283m** of depreciation.
- Hold the strategy link: heavy depreciation → low book value → **big gain on sale** → underwrites Singapore's young-fleet, fast-turnover model.
